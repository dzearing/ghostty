## In plain terms

The banner that floats above a pane is meant to look like a piece of glass
with a light shining down on it from above — brightest in the middle of its
top edge, fading off toward the two ends. On Windows the light was being
applied straight down the card instead, so a banner stretched across a wide
pane got one evenly-lit stripe along its whole top edge, which reads as a
painted band rather than as a lit surface. After this it catches the light
the way the Mac one does. The same card is used by a viewer pane's contents
list, so that gets it too.

## Decision: what Windows does instead of Liquid Glass

**Nothing new — the card stays hand-composited, and that IS the Windows
answer.** Mac's own `GlassCardBackground` is hand-drawn too, and deliberately:
its doc comment records that the system `glassEffect` re-renders on window
key-state changes, visibly shifting the card's color, and that its frost
washes the pane hue toward grey. Both objections apply verbatim to Mica and
Acrylic, which additionally sample the DESKTOP behind the window — a banner
floating inside a pane must be tinted off the PANE, not off whatever wallpaper
happens to be behind the terminal.

So the adopted items are the two that are about light rather than about
material: the elliptical sheen and the elliptical rim. `286078a2f`/`755af5c97`
are landed as gradients, not as a backdrop material, and no `DwmSetWindowAttribute`
backdrop is introduced.

## Validation criteria

      near the card's ends is measurably dimmer — the wide-banner stripe this
      task exists to remove. (`none` lane: "render: a wide card's top edge is
      a highlight, not a stripe", plus "sheenAlpha: bulges into the top-center

      lit at the gradient's first stop. (`none` lane: "Ellipse: the specular

      the same overhead ellipse as the sheen".)
